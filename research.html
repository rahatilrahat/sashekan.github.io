Working Papers

Labor Market Power in Indian Manufacturing

Job Market Paper

[Abstract]

How much labor market power do employers exercise in Indian manufacturing? I study this question using firm-level production data over 2000–2019. I measure employer market power using wage markdowns, defined as the ratio of the marginal revenue product of labor to the wage. Combining estimated firm-level production functions with payroll data, I find a median markdown of 1.56, implying that the marginal revenue product of labor is 56% higher than the wage at the median firm. Markdowns vary substantially in the cross-section of firms and the differences in revenue responses to changes in labor input make the largest positive contribution to the variance in log markdowns. Relating markdowns to firm characteristics, I find that higher productivity is associated with higher markdowns, while importing and exporting are associated with approximately 10.8% and 5.6% lower markdowns. At the macro level, I also document the trend in aggregate markdowns which peaked in 2006 and generally declined since.

Trade Shocks, Intermediate Inputs and Labor Market Power

New draft coming soon

[Abstract]

This paper studies the effect of increased exposure to import competition on monopsony power among employers. I estimate wage markdowns in a production framework and, exploiting the unprecedented rise in Chinese exports, investigate how they respond to trade shocks. Distinguishing greater access to imported intermediate inputs from tougher output market competition, I construct shift-share exposure measures using firms’ initial input and output baskets. Baseline estimates with fixed effects imply that a 10 p.p. increase in input access exposure reduces markdowns by about 10%, while an equivalent increase in output-competition exposure reduces them by 6%. Exploring the mechanism, I find evidence that the decline reflects lower output elasticity of labor and higher revenue share of payroll, partly offset by lower output markups.

Who Leaves Agriculture? Structural Transformation under Monopsony

[Summary]

In this project, I explore how monopsonistic labor markets in the non-agricultural sectors drive the movement of workers out of agriculture as economies develop. In a two-sector model with heterogenous workers, greater employer concentration outside agriculture lowers wages in those jobs and leads more workers to choose agricultural work. As productivity rises and agriculture shrinks, employers outside agriculture can gain wage-setting power even holding fixed the number of firms. I calibrate the model to U.S. data to quantify the effects of employer competition on employment and welfare, and compare its predictions for agricultural employment and productivity with cross-country evidence.
